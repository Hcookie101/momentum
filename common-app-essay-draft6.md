# Draft 6 — as pasted (Present Me / Future Me conceit)

"I'll finish this essay later." At 1:15 AM, the cursor on my blank Google Doc blinked like it agreed. I had said this same phrase after school, while putting the dinner plates away, and over a glass of water at 11. The Present Me focused on the short-term gains of just one more video, one more game, and gave the important, uncomfortable things to Future Me, thinking he would be more equipped to handle the task.

This wasn't new to me. I once procrastinated on a Statistics WebAssign worth a full test grade to the weekend, believing that delaying would unlock a temporary genius in Future Me. Instead, Future Me forgot it existed until 7:30 the next morning and was greeted by four cold words on a locked portal: No longer accepting submissions. The statistical probability of my strategy working was about 0, yet I couldn't help myself.

I knew I couldn't just rely on raw willpower. Willpower was like a weak muscle. It had a limited amount of power until it's exhausted and Present Me wins. I needed to force myself to keep the interests of Future Me in my priorities. I tried traditional productivity apps that helped me organize and create plans. My phone became a graveyard of color-coded calendars and pomodoro timers. Frustrated with my limited success, I opened up VS Code and made my own unified dashboard. Momentum. Yet this first version, just like the others, relied on using willpower to force Future Me to carry these usually impractical schedules.

I searched for answers, eventually realizing the issue lay in my perception of time. Research shows that when people think about their future selves, their brains light up in the same regions used when thinking about total strangers. In a way, I was outsourcing my work to a complete stranger, Future Me, so that Present Me didn't have to worry, only to realize that Future Me was just as exhausted and overwhelmed as I was.

So I rebuilt Momentum, this time focused on reducing the gap between the 2 strangers living in my mind. I added a focus timer, making starting tasks as simple as the click of a button.
I also added a stats page that collected data like browsing history and focus time collected through focus timer, as well as an integrated AI to analyze my habits. This meant that when Present Me was planning out the day, the decisions could be informed by past data, instead of assuming that Future Me would operate under perfect conditions with infinite energy. Seeing my focus hours or completed tasks also had the added bonus of giving Present Me a tangible short-term incentive for the work I put in that day, bridging the gap between present effort and future payoff, and making productivity addictive.

The difference showed up quickly. For months, my Eagle Scout project proposal sat radiating silent guilt from its untouched browser tab. Starting it felt overwhelming, so I kept putting it off to Future Me. Instead of burdening the future stranger with a massive task, Present Me clicked the focus timer to commit to just opening it. By eliminating the friction of starting, the entire first draft was finished before midnight.

Building Momentum reshaped how I view personal failure. Instead of viewing my character flaws as something to overcome through sheer willpower, I saw them as bugs in my system. Instead of wasting energy on blaming my discipline, I can locate the root cause, design a new system, and iterate until it works.

I still hear that "I'll finish later" from time to time. The difference now is I have an answer, using something I built. This essay was written inside a Momentum focus session between 10:47 PM and 1:14 AM.

The cursor still blinks. But Future Me isn't a stranger anymore, and Present Me doesn't always win.
